\section{Game Design, Models, and Development Process}

\subsection{Overall design logic}

The design goal of Aion's Edge was not to simulate a realistic colony in
full detail. Instead, the goal was to build a small but readable system
in which each gameplay stage corresponds to a different class of
decision problem from the course. This led to three design principles.

First, every level must expose a decision with visible constraints or
conflicts. Second, the consequence of a decision must carry into later
stages, so that optimisation is treated as a dynamic process rather than
as a one-shot calculation. Third, the interface must make the underlying
mathematical structure legible enough that a player can connect actions
to concepts such as feasibility, non-dominance, and social choice.

The resulting game loop is a four-phase turn structure: production,
policy, council, and resolution. The production stage generates
resources, the policy stage chooses a medium-term allocation, the council
stage decides which development plan is politically accepted, and the
resolution stage applies persistent changes to colony state variables
such as economy, environment, morale, oxygen reserve, and food reserve.
This persistence is important because it turns each local optimisation
problem into part of a longer decision process.

\subsection{Level 1: Linear programming as survival planning}

Level 1 frames the player as the colony AI responsible for producing
oxygen $x_1$ and food $x_2$ while respecting energy and labour limits.
The game presents the optimisation problem as

\[
\max Z = c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2
\]

subject to

\[
2x_1 + x_2 \leq b_1, \qquad x_1 + 2x_2 \leq b_2, \qquad x_1, x_2 \geq 0.
\]

This is a deliberately small model, but it captures the main idea of
linear programming: a linear objective is optimised over a feasible
region defined by linear constraints. In the
implementation, the solver uses \texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog} with
the HiGHS backend. The player sees the feasible polygon, the chosen
point, and the optimal point, so the geometry of the model remains
visible during play.

To avoid making the level feel like a static worksheet, I added random
events that modify the right-hand side vector or objective coefficients.
For example, a dust storm reduces available energy, a flu outbreak
reduces labour, and a technology breakthrough increases the production
value coefficients. These events show that the same model structure can
lead to different feasible regions and different optima when parameters
change.

The scoring logic also follows the model. A feasible plan yields output
and improves longer-term colony variables in proportion to efficiency
relative to the LP optimum. An infeasible plan causes reserve losses and
morale damage. This means the level rewards both feasibility and quality
of allocation.

\subsection{Level 2: Multi-objective optimisation as policy design}

Level 2 shifts from a single-objective optimisation problem to a
multi-objective design problem. The player allocates a fixed budget
among industry, ecology, and welfare, and each allocation is mapped to
two main objectives: pollution and economic output. A third indicator,
morale lift, influences the persistent game state. The player proposal
is then compared against AI-generated alternatives sampled from a
Dirichlet distribution.

This level operationalises a central course idea: in multi-objective
optimisation there is usually no single best solution. Instead, we look
for the set of non-dominated solutions known as the Pareto front
In the implementation, pollution is a
minimisation objective and output is a maximisation objective, so the
solver normalises directions before checking dominance. The player can
therefore see whether a chosen policy is Pareto-optimal or dominated.
If it is dominated, the interface points to an AI policy that dominates
it. This makes the definition of dominance easy to understand in
practice.

The level also computes the Nadir point of the Pareto front. This gives
a worst-case reference among efficient policies and supports discussion
of ideal and anti-ideal thinking in multi-criteria analysis. The main
message of the level is simple: a good policy is not one perfect point,
but a trade-off on a frontier.

Another design choice is that Pareto-optimal policies reward the player
with an influence token used in the later voting stage. This links
mathematical efficiency to political leverage. Efficient policy design
helps in the next stage, but it does not guarantee final adoption.

\subsection{Level 3: Voting theory and MCDA in the council}

Level 3 turns the policy problem into a collective choice problem. Three
factions with different priorities must rank three development plans,
and the final winner depends on the voting rule. The game implements
plurality, Borda Count, and Condorcet comparison, which are standard
examples for discussing preference aggregation. The weights of the
factions are endogenous: they depend on the current colony state. This
means earlier optimisation decisions influence not only resources but
also the political structure of the next stage.

This level reflects two lessons from the course. First, aggregation
rules matter: the same profile of preferences can generate different
winners under different rules. Second, collective rationality is not
guaranteed: Condorcet cycles can emerge, illustrating the instability
of majority-based comparison. The
project includes both a live council mode and paradox-oriented
scenarios. In the live mode, the player may spend influence tokens to
lobby a faction and alter its top-ranked plan.

From an MCDA perspective, this stage shows the gap between objective
evaluation and institutional choice. A plan that looks attractive in
objective terms may still lose once preferences are aggregated.

\subsection{How the project was refined step by step}

The project did not begin as a fully integrated educational game. It
started with two neuroevolution exercises, Flappy Bird and Lunar
Lander. These earlier experiments established a practical workflow for
experimentation and improvement and helped shape the broader idea that
optimisation should be visible through interaction rather than hidden in
tables of results.

The next major step was the introduction of Aion's Edge as a three-level
Streamlit game. At that point, the project already contained the main
ideas of the course, but the implementation was concentrated in a very
large entry point and one monolithic optimisation engine. This was a
useful prototype because it made the game playable early, but it also
made the structure difficult to maintain and explain.

The next problem, therefore, was not whether the game worked, but how to
make its logic clearer. I addressed this in stages. First, the
application was split into campaign and level modules so that global
state and level-specific behaviour were no longer mixed together.
Second, the turn flow was reorganised into production, policy, council,
and resolution, which made the player experience easier to follow and
aligned the gameplay more closely with the conceptual sequence of the
course. Third, campaign state, level logic, and view code were separated
into packages. Finally, the former monolithic optimisation engine was
replaced with dedicated modules for linear programming,
multi-objective optimisation, and voting. At that point, the software
structure matched the conceptual structure much more closely.

This progression reflects two kinds of learning. The first is modelling
learning: the course ideas become more explicit in the design. The
second is software engineering learning: a working prototype is
gradually turned into a more modular and readable codebase. The report
module is part of that same progression because it turns the repository
into a documented course submission rather than a code-only artefact.
