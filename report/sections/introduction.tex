\section{Introduction}

This project was developed for the course ``Multi-Criteria Optimization
and Decision Analysis'' with the goal of showing that operations
research can be communicated through an interactive system rather than
only through static exercises. Instead of presenting linear programming,
multi-objective optimisation, and voting theory as separate textbook
topics, I designed a small strategy game in which each turn asks the
player to solve one decision problem from the course.

The main artefact is \emph{Aion's Edge}, a Streamlit-based colony
management game. The player acts as the governing AI of a fragile
settlement and must survive a sequence of turns. Each turn is divided
into three decision stages. In Level 1, the player chooses production of
oxygen and food under energy and labour constraints, which creates a
classical linear-programming problem. In Level 2, the player allocates a
policy budget among industry, ecology, and welfare, and the resulting
proposal is evaluated against AI-generated alternatives through Pareto
dominance and Nadir analysis. In Level 3, the policy direction must be
converted into law by a council of factions whose preferences are
aggregated by plurality, Borda Count, or Condorcet comparison. The game
therefore links optimisation, trade-offs, and political feasibility in a
single loop.

This design reflects a core lesson from the course: real decisions are
rarely single-objective. A mathematically good action can still be
socially blocked, and a politically acceptable action can still be
dominated in objective space. By placing these tensions into a playable
sequence, the project makes explicit why decision analysis is broader
than pure optimisation. Linear programming is used to find efficient
allocations; multi-objective optimisation is used to
reason about non-dominated trade-offs; and voting
theory is used to reveal how collective choice can change when the
aggregation rule changes. The game is therefore not
just inspired by the course content; it is structured by it.

The repository also contains two earlier neuroevolution exercises,
Flappy Bird with a custom genetic algorithm and Lunar Lander with NEAT.
Those experiments are relevant because they shaped the way I approached
search and improvement in the main game. They were my first attempts to
make optimisation visible through behaviour rather than through numeric
tables alone. However, the centre of this report is Aion's Edge because
it is the part that most directly integrates the course topics into one
design.

The remainder of the report has two purposes. First, it explains the
game design and the operations research concepts behind each stage.
Second, it explains how the project gradually evolved from early
experiments into a clearer and more coherent course project. Since this
is a course assignment, that second part is important: I want to show
not only what the final result is, but also how course knowledge guided
the modelling and software-design choices that produced it. The project
repository is available at
\url{https://github.com/lithegreat/Aion-s-Edge}.
