\section{Conclusion}

This project shows one way to turn operations research from an abstract
set of methods into a sequence of concrete decisions. In Aion's Edge,
linear programming becomes a problem of feasible survival planning,
multi-objective optimisation becomes a problem of balancing economic and
environmental trade-offs, and voting theory becomes a problem of turning
analysis into collective action. That progression was deliberate. It was
my attempt to represent the course not as three unrelated techniques,
but as a pipeline from optimisation to decision support to institutional
choice.

The project improved through identifiable stages: early
experiments with neuroevolution, a first integrated game prototype, and
then a sequence of refinements that made the codebase better aligned
with the decision models it implements. In that sense, the repository
shows both conceptual learning and engineering learning. I was not only
using course methods to solve game problems; I was also learning how to
shape software so that the underlying methods remain visible and
explainable.

The current version still has limitations. The mathematical models are
intentionally small, which supports teaching but limits realism.
Uncertainty is represented through a few scenario events rather than a
full stochastic model. The multi-objective stage uses sampled AI
alternatives rather than solving a continuous optimisation problem over a
more realistic policy space. The voting stage illustrates paradoxes well,
but it does not yet include richer MCDA tools such as weighted scoring,
outranking, or interactive preference elicitation. These limitations are
acceptable for a course project, but they also point to meaningful next
steps.
